About this map

Every tree in the City of Toronto's street tree inventory — about 688,000 of them — coloured by whether it is native to Ontario, by trunk size, or by species.

Native or not is the default, and it has four tiers, not two: native to Ontario, native to eastern North America, introduced, and introduced and invasive. Norway maple alone is 69,474 trees — a tenth of every street tree in the city, and invasive. Compare species picks out up to three at a time. Trunk size shades by diameter, which reads as a map of tree age.

Where the data comes from

The City's Street Tree Data, refreshed weekly, with ward boundaries for the overlay. Native status is resolved per species against a curated list of Ontario-native trees. The code that builds it is on GitHub.

Caveats worth knowing

This is City-owned trees on the road allowance — not Toronto's whole canopy. Park interiors, ravines and back yards are absent, which is why the map thins out over the Don Valley and High Park.

Positions are geocoded to the street address, not surveyed at the trunk, so a dot sits on the parcel rather than on the tree. Every tree at one address lands on the same point.

Native to eastern North America is its own tier, not a kind of introduced: a tree whose wild range lies east of roughly the Mississippi, with that line carried north into Canada, but which never reached Ontario. Honey locust and black locust live here — near-natives that belong to this forest type without belonging to this place. Colorado blue spruce and Douglas fir stay introduced; they are as foreign here as a European import, just from the other side of the continent.

A tree the City recorded only to genus stays “not identified to species” rather than being guessed at — Acer alone could be a sugar maple or a Norway maple. That is 43,273 trees, reported as their own category instead of being folded into any of the others.
