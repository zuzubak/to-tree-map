Every census area in Toronto, plotted by how many people live on a hectare against how many street trees stand on 100 m of its streets. The line is the average of each bin along the axis; hover it to read any density.

The shape of it

Not much, until it does. The average climbs gently to about 15 trees per 100 m around 20 residents a hectare, runs flat near 13.5 through the whole middle of the city, and then falls away above roughly 200 — reaching about 8 per 100 m in the densest bin.

The fall at the top is the only part with real movement in it. Apartment towers set back behind parking and podiums have less street frontage to plant, and what frontage there is tends to be arterial.

A straight line through this cloud would be nearly flat and would explain about 1% of the variation between areas — true, but it would only be telling you that no straight line describes it. The binned average is drawn instead, with a mark at each bin, so the shape comes from the data rather than from a model of it.

How this is measured

One point per census dissemination area, because that is the only unit that can carry both axes. Population is published there and nowhere finer that is usable, so asking this of a street block would mean giving every block in an area the same density and drawing 39,000 points in 3,565 vertical stripes.

Street length is clipped to each area's own boundary rather than assigned whole, so a street running along the edge between two areas counts half to each. Areas holding less than 200 m of street are left out: a stub of road through a ravine with four trees on it is not a densely planted place. So are areas with no residents, which have no density to plot.

Former municipality comes from the City's own boundary file, by largest overlap. Census areas do not nest inside the 1998 boundaries, and ward names only look like they encode them — Eglinton-Lawrence spans old Toronto and North York.

What this cannot tell you

Street trees are not canopy. This counts trees the City inventories on the street allowance. Private front gardens, parks and institutional land hold a great deal of Toronto's canopy and none of it is here, which matters most in exactly the low-density areas on the left of the chart.

Density is residents per hectare of the whole area, ravines and rail yards included, so a neighbourhood wrapped around a valley reads as sparser than it feels on the street.

The x axis is logarithmic because density spans four and a half decades. It stops at three clean ones, so area sparser than 1 resident per hectare and denser than 1,000 sit pinned to the edges rather than being given two more decades of empty chart. areas are planted more densely than trees per 100 m and sit on the top edge for the same reason. Hover reads the true figure in every case.

Six colours cannot be made colourblind-safe. Checked as all pairs in OKLab under simulated deuteranopia, protanopia and tritanopia, the best six of this site's palette still separate by less than the usual floor.
