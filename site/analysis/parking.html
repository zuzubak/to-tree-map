The short answer

It does not — if anything the opposite. Areas with no licensed front-yard parking average about 13.7 street trees per 100 m. Areas with the most average closer to 17.

That reads backwards until you notice the two things sit on different land. A front-yard permit covers private ground between the house and the sidewalk; a street tree stands on the public allowance beyond it. Paving a front yard does not remove a City tree, and the streets where people bother to license parking — narrow old-city lots with no driveway and no garage — are also the streets with the oldest and densest planting.

Read this one carefully

The x axis is partly a map of which bylaw you live under. Front-yard parking licensing is a legacy of the old City of Toronto, York and East York. Those three hold of every licensed space in the city. In North York, Etobicoke and Scarborough almost nobody applies, because the houses were built with driveways and garages and need no permit.

So areas — of the chart — sit at zero, and most of them are suburban. Citywide, moving right along this axis partly means moving into the old city. Isolate Toronto, York or East York below to see the relationship inside the places where the permit actually exists; the shape barely changes, which is the useful part.

How this is measured

permits covering licensed spaces, from the City's Residential Front Yard Parking register: front yard, widened driveway and boulevard spaces, all counted.

The register gives a street number and name but no coordinates. Each permit is placed on the block whose address range contains its number — the centreline publishes those ranges per side, so no geocoding service is involved — which places of licensed spaces. A permit sits at one house, so it is given to the area holding most of its block rather than split along it the way street length is.

Both axes are divided by the same street length, clipped to each area's own boundary, so they are directly comparable. Areas holding less than 200 m of street are left out.

What this cannot tell you

Licensed is not the same as paved. This is the permit register, not a survey of front gardens. Unlicensed parking pads exist and are invisible here, and a lapsed permit on a still-paved yard is too.

Street trees are not canopy. The thing a paved front yard most plausibly costs you is a private tree, and no private tree is in this dataset. A flat line here is not evidence that front-yard parking is harmless to the urban forest; it is evidence that it does not show up in the City's street tree inventory.

This is an association between areas, not a history. Nothing here says which came first on any given street.
