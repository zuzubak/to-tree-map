How the age is estimated

There is no planting date in the inventory, so age is estimated from trunk diameter by the arborist's standard field method: diameter in inches × a growth factor for the species, where the factor is the average number of years that species takes to add an inch of trunk.

Where each factor came from, by tree:

The published factors describe open-grown landscape trees. A street tree in a four-foot pit with compacted soil and road salt thickens more slowly than that, so these figures run young on the most stressed sites.

What this cannot tell you

It is an estimate shaped like a measurement. Treat a single block as an indication and the city-wide pattern as the finding.

The input is no better than the method. Recorded diameters heap on round numbers, and 164,966 trees — nearly a quarter of the inventory — sit on exactly 4 cm or 8 cm, which is a field convention rather than fifty thousand identical trunks. That flattens the young end of every distribution on this page.

Arithmetic on a bad diameter produces a bad age, and a slow-growing species multiplies the error. 343 trees came out older than 250 years — a 203 cm yew, a 250 cm hawthorn, neither of which exists — and are excluded from every figure here.
